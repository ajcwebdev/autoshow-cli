## Draft

Every live event has a moment where the technology reminds everyone who is in charge. For the latest JavaScript Jam Live session, that moment arrived right at the start. The Twitter Space showed as live on the host's phone, but nobody else could see it or join. The fix turned out to be patience: wait a minute or two, and the Space eventually works itself out and goes live. It is a small lesson for anyone hosting Twitter Spaces, and it set a good tone for the rest of the conversation. Things break, people laugh, and the show goes on. That attitude is exactly what the community around JavaScript Jam Live has been building, and it sits at the center of a new collaboration with React Miami.

What is JavaScript Jam Live?

JavaScript Jam Live is an open mic for anything related to JavaScript and web development. The rule is simple: if it touches JavaScript or the web, it is on topic. The hosts come from Edgio, an edge deployment platform. Ishan Anand is VP of Product, Anthony Campolo is a developer advocate, and Scott Steinlage is a technical community manager. Together they run the show, but the real fuel is audience participation. Beginners and veterans are equally welcome to request the mic, ask questions, share opinions, or toss out a fun fact. As Scott put it, when listeners get involved, some of the best conversations happen, and that raises the value for everyone tuned in.

The React Miami connection

The big news is a partnership with React Miami. Edgio's JavaScript Jam team is serving as a media partner for the conference, alongside Dev Agrawal, who joined the Space to talk about what is coming. Michelle, one of the React Miami organizers, also hopped on as a speaker once her connection and microphone button cooperated. She described the arrangement with a lot of trust: the organizers essentially gave the media partners the green light to do whatever they like, and she was just as curious as the audience to see what would come out of it.

Scott even renamed the occasion on the spot. What was billed as React Miami Week became React Miami Month, since the collaboration will stretch across several upcoming sessions. In the weeks ahead, speakers from React Miami will appear on JavaScript Jam Live to discuss topics that fit the community. Importantly, nothing about the traditional JavaScript Jam vibe is going to change. The same open, welcoming style continues, with extra value layered on top, including some goodies the hosts promised to share later.

Enter Miami Jam

The most interesting idea from the session is a game show called Miami Jam, which Dev Agrawal has roped Anthony and Scott into hosting alongside him. It is scheduled for the following week, and the premise is a deliberate departure from the usual conference-adjacent content.

Most speaker promotion follows a familiar pattern. You bring on a few speakers, ask them about their talk topics and motivations, and wrap up. It works, but it can feel predictable. Miami Jam swaps the straight interview for trivia-style questions. Those questions can be about React itself, about odd quirks in JavaScript or TypeScript, or about anything adjacent to the React ecosystem. A round might ask contestants to guess which library is being used based on a clue. Another might direct a question at one speaker about a different speaker's talk, which forces cross-pollination between the guests.

The point is not to quiz people for the sake of it. Each question is a launchpad. Once a speaker answers, or fumbles an answer, the hosts can segue into a deeper discussion of the speaker's topic, what drove them to give the talk, and what experiences in the industry brought them to React Miami. The audience still gets real insight from the speakers. The difference is that the insight arrives in a format that is more fun, more interactive, and more collaborative.

Audience participation built in

Another key ingredient is that listeners are not just spectators. If you are in the audience for the show, you can engage with the speakers in several ways. Naturally, you can ask your own questions. But you may also get a say in the scoring. When a question is asked, whether by the hosts or by you, the audience can decide how many points the speaker earns for answering it. That small mechanic hands real influence to the crowd and makes every answer a shared moment rather than a one-way transmission.

The hosts also want the speakers to trade experiences with each other, not just with the hosts. A game format makes that easier. People who might hesitate to ask a peer a pointed technical question in a formal panel will happily challenge them with a trivia puzzle. Friendly competition lowers the stakes and loosens everyone up, which tends to produce more candid stories and better technical discussion.

Why this format matters

Developer events can sometimes feel like a stream of polished talks with little room for personality. A game show format pushes against that. It rewards curiosity over rehearsed answers, it makes room for humor, and it gives the audience a reason to stay engaged for the entire session. It also fits the spirit of the Twitter Space that introduced it. The session began with a technical mishap, and everyone handled it with good humor. A game show built on quirks, surprises, and a little chaos feels like a natural extension of that energy.

There is a practical benefit for the conference too. Attendees get to preview speakers in a relaxed setting, learn who they are beyond the abstract of a talk, and decide which sessions they want to see in Miami. For speakers, it is a low-pressure warm-up that builds rapport with the community before the event itself.

How to get involved

If you want to participate, the invitation is open. Join JavaScript Jam Live, request the mic, and bring your questions, comments, facts, or opinions. Whether you have been building for the web for a few months or a few decades, your voice is wanted. Keep an eye out for the Miami Jam game show next week, and for the speaker appearances scheduled throughout React Miami Month.

The larger lesson is that community events do not need to follow a rigid template. With a bit of creativity, a trivia round can be as revealing as a keynote Q and A. And if the technology stumbles along the way, as Twitter Spaces occasionally does, a little patience and a sense of humor will usually get the show going.
