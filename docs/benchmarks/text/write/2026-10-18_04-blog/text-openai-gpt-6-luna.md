Turning a React Conference Preview Into a Game Show: The Miami Jam Idea

- What the format could offer speakers, attendees, and listeners

## Draft

Live technology events rarely go exactly to plan. In one JavaScript Jam Live conversation, the hosts began by talking through a Twitter Spaces hiccup: the session appeared to be starting on one person’s phone, but other listeners could not yet see or join it. After a short wait, the Space came online. The hosts treated the delay as a useful reminder that patience is sometimes part of the job when working with live tools—and then got on with the conversation.

That small moment set the tone for the rest of the event. JavaScript Jam Live is an open-mic gathering for people interested in JavaScript, web development, and the ideas surrounding them. The hosts encourage audience participation, and the session’s focus was a new collaboration with React Miami. Rather than simply announcing a conference partnership, the group introduced an event concept designed to bring speakers and audience members into a more lively exchange: Miami Jam.

## An open mic for the web community

JavaScript Jam Live is built around a broad idea of what counts as a worthwhile conversation. Beginners and experienced developers alike are welcome to request the mic, ask questions, share opinions, or contribute something they have learned. As host Scott Steinlage explained, the goal is to hear from people at different stages of their development journeys. A room with varied experience can lead to questions and perspectives that might not arise in a more formal presentation.

The hosts also emphasized that this open format would continue through their React Miami collaboration. The partnership was meant to add opportunities and content for the community, not to change the familiar character of JavaScript Jam Live. React Miami speakers would join future conversations to discuss subjects connected to their work, while the usual spirit of audience involvement would remain.

That balance matters. Conference programming can introduce people to new ideas, but the most memorable conversations often happen when attendees can respond, ask follow-up questions, and connect a speaker’s subject to their own experience. An open-mic format makes that kind of participation part of the event rather than an afterthought.

## A different kind of speaker conversation

The centerpiece of the discussion was Miami Jam, a show Dev Agrawal proposed hosting with Anthony Campolo and Scott. The idea is to move away from the familiar interview or panel structure. Instead of beginning with a list of direct questions about a speaker’s topic, the hosts plan to use trivia-style prompts as a way into the conversation.

The questions could cover React, unusual JavaScript or TypeScript behavior, or related topics. They might ask someone to identify a library, or invite a speaker to answer a question connected to another person’s subject. The question is not intended to replace a substantive discussion. It is a starting point: a playful prompt that can lead into a closer look at a talk, a speaker’s motivation, or an experience from their career.

That structure could make it easier for speakers to interact with one another, rather than taking turns delivering isolated answers. A question directed at one person may reveal a connection to another speaker’s work. From there, the hosts can follow the thread and let the conversation develop. The result is still a chance to hear meaningful context and insight, but the route to that insight is more spontaneous than a standard panel format.

The game-show approach also gives the event a clear sense of momentum. Trivia questions provide a shared activity for speakers and listeners, and they offer a natural way to introduce technical ideas without making the whole session feel like a lecture. A question about a library or a language quirk can spark an explanation, a story, or a debate about how developers solve a particular problem.

## Making room for the audience

Audience involvement is central to the plan. The listeners are not expected to sit quietly while speakers answer questions; they will have opportunities to ask their own. Dev also described ways the audience might participate in the game itself. For example, listeners could have a say in how many points a speaker receives for an answer.

The source conversation ends while Dev is explaining another possible audience interaction, so the full set of game mechanics was not laid out. Even so, the intention was clear: the audience should help shape the experience. That can make a technical event feel more collaborative and less like a performance delivered from a stage.

Participation can also benefit people who are newer to a subject. A beginner’s question may surface an assumption that more experienced developers take for granted. Conversely, a question from a specialist can draw out details that help the whole room understand a topic more deeply. When people feel invited to contribute, the discussion can serve a wider range of attendees.

## A collaboration with room to experiment

React Miami organizer Michelle joined the conversation and welcomed the media partners involved in the collaboration. She explained that the group had been given room to develop its own ideas, which made Miami Jam an opportunity to experiment with a different style of conference programming.

That freedom is reflected in the concept. Miami Jam is not simply another preview interview. It combines technical trivia, speaker stories, audience questions, and the possibility of interaction between guests. The format aims to make the event entertaining while still giving speakers space to explain what they are working on and why it matters to them.

The hosts described the React Miami partnership as a source of additional value for the community, including more speakers and activities. Their invitation was broad: whether someone is just beginning to explore web development or has been building for years, they are welcome to listen, ask a question, or request the mic.

## Join the conversation

The Miami Jam idea offers a useful reminder that technical learning does not have to follow only one format. Presentations and traditional panels can be valuable, but a game-show structure may help people relax, connect ideas, and discover common ground. A trivia prompt can be a doorway into a serious discussion; a playful exchange can make room for the kind of detail that a scripted interview might miss.

Most of all, the plan reflects the community-first spirit the JavaScript Jam Live hosts described. The event is designed not only for speakers to share their work, but also for listeners to take part. For anyone interested in React, JavaScript, TypeScript, or the broader web development community, Miami Jam promises a different way to meet ideas and people. And, as the opening Twitter Spaces delay showed, a little patience—and a willingness to adapt—can help a live conversation find its footing.
