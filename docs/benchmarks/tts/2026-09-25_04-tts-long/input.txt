The blue lantern was still burning when Mara reached the northern gate. Beyond it, the road curved around the hill toward the old coastal observatory, where a narrow window glowed above the sea. She stopped to fasten her coat. The morning ferry had carried her through rain, the afternoon bus had left her beside an empty shelter, and the final mile had been longer than the map suggested. Now it was 6:47 p.m. on Thursday, October 15, and the only person waiting at the gate was a man holding a yellow umbrella upside down.

“It catches the rain,” he said, before she could ask. “Unfortunately, that is exactly the problem.” His name was Elias Bell. He was the observatory's caretaker, occasional cook, and unofficial keeper of everything that had lost its label. He turned the umbrella over, shook it twice, and offered to carry her smaller bag. Mara kept the larger one. Inside were a notebook, a digital recorder, two spare batteries, and a photograph of a woman standing beside this same gate forty years earlier. The woman in the photograph was her grandmother. The lantern above her had been blue then, too.

They walked uphill together. The observatory had once measured the stars, then the weather, then the slow movement of the shore. Each change had brought a new sign, a new department, and another collection of instruments that somebody hoped to repair. The stone building remained stubbornly itself. Its west wall faced the open water; its east windows overlooked a sheltered garden; its northern door stuck whenever the wind came from the south. Elias explained these facts with the patient satisfaction of someone introducing an old friend whose faults had become part of his appeal.

Mara had come to record the final overnight watch before the building closed for renovation. The instruments would move inland the following week. The handwritten journals would go to the town archive, the furniture to a warehouse, and the enormous brass telescope to a workshop where, according to the proposal, it would receive a comprehensive mechanical assessment. “That means they haven't decided how to get it through the door,” Elias said. Mara looked back at the road. From this height, the blue lantern appeared no larger than a match flame, but she could still distinguish it from every other light along the shore.

Inside, a clock struck seven, followed several seconds later by a second clock that seemed reluctant to agree. Dr. Leila Rahman met them in the entrance hall. She had a pencil behind one ear and a strip of masking tape attached to her sleeve. On the tape she had written, in small, neat letters, the words west cabinet, lower shelf. “I keep finding labels on myself,” she said. “At this rate, the movers will put me in storage.” She showed Mara a table beside the kitchen and asked her to leave the recorder there until everyone had finished eating.

Dinner was lentil soup, thick bread, and a salad assembled from the last useful things in the refrigerator. Niamh O'Sullivan arrived carrying a bowl of roasted peppers; Tomás Alvarez brought six mismatched spoons; and a graduate student named Anika Das appeared with an unopened jar of mustard that nobody had requested. They made room for her anyway. Niamh maintained the electronic sensors. Tomás cared for the paper records. Anika was studying the relationship between local wind patterns and the shape of the headland. Leila supervised the station, although Elias insisted that the station mostly supervised Leila.

The conversation moved easily from weather to plumbing to the difficulty of ordering replacement parts for equipment older than its manufacturer. Mara listened before turning on her recorder. She wanted to learn their ordinary rhythms: where they paused, which stories they interrupted, and how a roomful of people made space for a quieter voice. When she finally asked what she should remember about the place, Anika answered first. “The windows,” she said. “Every room gives you a different version of the same evening.” Outside the kitchen, the garden lay almost still. Through the western corridor, the sea looked as though it were trying to climb the glass.

After dinner, they began with the inventory. There were 24 working thermometers, 9 damaged rain gauges, 3 unopened cartons of chart paper, and 1 wooden box whose contents had defeated two previous inventories. The box weighed 8.75 kilograms. Its label listed a purchase price of £126.40, followed by a handwritten question mark. Beneath that, someone had added a second figure, £162.40, and crossed it out. Tomás proposed recording the object as a box containing uncertainty. Leila asked him to open it. Inside were twelve replacement handles, individually wrapped, for a cabinet that had been removed in 1983.

“First observation,” Mara said into the recorder. “The station has prepared thoroughly for an event that already happened.” She waited for the others to finish laughing before continuing. Her grandmother, Ruth Vale, had worked here as a junior observer. Ruth rarely spoke about the instruments. She remembered a kettle that whistled in two different keys, a supervisor who could identify colleagues by their footsteps, and the smell of wet wool drying above a radiator. Mara had expected these details to feel small beside the building. Instead, the building seemed to have grown around them.

At 8:16 p.m., Niamh invited her into the instrument room. Three screens showed nearly identical graphs, but each represented a different stretch of coastline. The northern sensor reported 12.6 degrees Celsius. The harbor sensor reported 12.8. The third displayed 128, which was sufficiently dramatic to make Mara look twice. “A missing decimal point,” Niamh said. “The water hasn't suddenly become a saucepan.” She opened a maintenance note dated 03/04/2025, frowned, and checked the heading. The author had used day, month, year. Niamh wrote out April 3 in full before adding her own comment.

One graph rose steadily, another fluttered, and the third remained almost flat. Niamh explained that a flat line could mean calm conditions, a disconnected cable, an exhausted battery, or a perfectly healthy instrument measuring something that barely changed. The shape alone did not settle the question. She compared the latest reading with a second sensor, checked the time stamp, and walked to the window. “The first rule of working beside a window,” she said, “is occasionally to look through it.” A gust pressed rain against the glass. Somewhere overhead, a loose fitting began to tap.

The tapping came in irregular groups: three quick beats, a pause, one beat, and then silence. Elias identified it as the drain bracket above the upper landing. Tomás believed it was the shutter on the telescope room. Anika suggested that both could be right. This led to a discussion of whether an old building produced separate noises or one enormous conversation. Mara recorded the sound for thirty seconds. When they listened back, the microphone had also caught the refrigerator, the clock, the wind, and Elias quietly asking where he had left his tea.

By nine o'clock, the observatory had settled into its nighttime routine. Leila checked the forecast. Anika updated the watch log. Niamh examined a spare connector beneath a desk lamp, turning it slowly between her fingers. Tomás spread several journals across the archive table, placing a clean sheet beneath each one. Mara sat beside him. The oldest volume had a green cloth cover and a spine repaired with brown tape. Its pages smelled faintly of dust and sea air. On the first page, a careful hand had written the station's name, followed by the year 1948 and a reminder to close the book before opening the window.

The archive revealed itself through details that never appeared in official summaries. A storm entry recorded wind direction, air pressure, cloud cover, and the fact that somebody had borrowed the good saucepan. Another described a clear night with unusually sharp stars, then apologized for an ink blot shaped like a rabbit. A third listed the arrival of a replacement barometer at 11:05 a.m., the discovery that its glass was cracked at 11:12, and the departure of the delivery van at 11:03. Tomás let Mara consider the sequence before explaining that the station clock had been running fourteen minutes fast.

“Here is your grandmother,” he said at last. Ruth's first entry occupied six lines near the bottom of a page. Her handwriting was smaller than Mara expected, and the letters leaned slightly to the right. She had recorded a temperature of 7.4 degrees, visibility of approximately 6 nautical miles, and a thin bank of cloud moving northeast. Then came a sentence outside the ruled columns. The lamp at the northern gate appears blue from the road and almost white from the hill. Mara read it twice. A description she had thought belonged to her own arrival had been waiting here since before she was born.

Tomás turned to a later entry, where Ruth described a visitor from Worcestershire who had arrived carrying a damaged violin and a letter addressed to the chief observer. Nobody knew why the violin mattered. The visitor's name appeared once as Mr. Beauchamp and once as Mr. Beacham. The chief observer had underlined both spellings and written verify beneath them. Mara asked whether the mystery had ever been resolved. Tomás shook his head. Archives, he said, were full of questions that had survived the people qualified to answer them. Sometimes the honest conclusion was a blank space with very good handwriting around it.

They continued reading while the weather thickened outside, moving from a page about a broken hinge to a page about a visiting school, from there to an account of a winter morning when the steps had vanished beneath snow, and onward through a succession of ordinary watches whose small differences slowly became more interesting than any single dramatic event: the afternoon when the sea looked green instead of gray, the week when a robin nested above the entrance, the evening when a new observer mistook the reflection of a desk lamp for an approaching boat, the long summer during which everybody complained about the heat and then complained about the rain that ended it, and the September night when Ruth, still young enough to believe that every mistake would be remembered forever, spilled half a cup of tea across the previous day's measurements, carefully copied the readable figures onto a fresh sheet, described the damage in the margin, and returned the following morning to find that her supervisor had added only four words beneath her explanation: thank you for saying.

Mara stopped at those words. She had known her grandmother as a person who checked doors twice and returned borrowed books early. It was strange to imagine her frightened by a cup of tea. Yet the page made that younger person suddenly clear. The record contained an error, a repair, and somebody's decision to make honesty easier. Tomás left the book open while Mara described the passage aloud. She spoke slowly enough to distinguish the original entry from her own response, then allowed a few seconds of quiet to remain on the recording.

At 10:23 p.m., the telephone rang. Leila answered in the hall. The voice on the other end belonged to the harbor office, where a routine update had been delayed by an equipment fault. No one at the observatory seemed especially alarmed, but the atmosphere changed. Chairs moved back. Cups were set down. Niamh checked the clock on her laptop against the clock above the doorway and wrote the difference on a scrap of paper. The two clocks were now eleven seconds apart. Mara watched the pencil travel across the page and understood that the evening had become something more exacting than a farewell visit.

The harbor office needed confirmation of a water-level reading. Their western instrument showed 1.82 meters above the reference mark, while the eastern instrument showed 1.28. The figures had the same digits in a different order. Either value was possible; the disagreement was the problem. Leila asked for the time of each observation, the units, and the reference point. She repeated the answers before ending the call. Then she turned to the others. “We have a question about a measurement,” she said. “Let's keep it that size until the evidence gives us a reason to change it.”

Niamh and Anika went to the instrument room. Elias checked that the lower corridor windows were secure. Tomás covered the journals and moved them farther from the outer wall. Mara remained at the kitchen table, where the recorder's small red light continued to glow. She could hear individual words through the open door: pressure, offset, battery, yesterday. Then a longer sentence became clear. “The number is plausible, but the time stamp belongs to an earlier transmission.” A chair scraped across the floor. Somebody asked for the original file. Somebody else said they had found it.

The next hour unfolded in one long, patient investigation. Niamh began with the newest message from the harbor instrument and worked backward, comparing its time stamp with the previous message, then the message before that, then the separate record kept by the relay on the hill. Anika read the values aloud while Leila entered them into a notebook, leaving enough room between rows to add corrections without squeezing figures into the margins. They found a brief interruption shortly after 9:40 p.m., followed by a burst of delayed transmissions that had arrived in an unexpected order. At first, the explanation seemed complete. An older reading had been displayed as though it were current. Yet the second instrument still differed from the observatory's own measurements by more than the usual amount, so Niamh resisted the temptation to close the case. She checked the sensor's reference setting. Leila called the harbor office again and asked them to read the label beside the eastern display. The label gave a date, a serial number, and a reference height that belonged to an earlier installation. There was a pause at the other end of the line, followed by the sound of a drawer opening. A newer calibration sheet existed, but it had been filed beneath the manual for a printer. This prompted Elias to observe that printers had probably concealed more useful information than they had ever produced. Nobody answered immediately. Anika was comparing two columns of figures, and Mara could see her lips moving as she counted the intervals. Twelve minutes separated one pair of readings. Eighteen separated the next. Then came six minutes, six minutes, and twenty-four. The irregular spacing mattered because the water had been rising throughout the period; a comparison between unmatched times could suggest a disagreement that disappeared when the clocks were aligned. They copied the readings onto separate sheets and placed those sheets side by side, first arranging them by arrival time and then by observation time. The pattern changed. A sharp jump became a steady slope. A supposed drop became a late message. The instruments had not been telling incompatible stories so much as telling the same story with their pages shuffled. Still, Leila wanted an independent check. She asked Elias whether the sheltered gauge near the garden could be read from the covered landing. He said it could, provided someone remembered to bring the small flashlight rather than the large one, whose switch had developed a philosophical objection to being pressed. Anika fetched the small flashlight. Elias put on his coat. They went only as far as the landing, leaving the outer door open behind them, and returned with a reading and the exact time at which they had taken it. Niamh added the observation to her graph. It sat close to the corrected trend. She did not announce a triumph. She simply leaned back, looked again at the screen, and said that the separate measurements now agreed within their expected uncertainty. Leila relayed that conclusion to the harbor office, including the remaining uncertainty and the correction to the reference setting. The person on the telephone repeated the figures back. Anika checked each one against the notebook. Only after both sides agreed did Leila end the call. The room became quiet enough for Mara to notice that the tapping overhead had stopped. She had not heard it stop, and she could not say whether it had been silent for a minute or twenty minutes. The investigation had drawn everyone's attention toward a narrow collection of numbers while the larger night continued around them. Rain still crossed the windows. Water still moved along the shore. The clocks remained eleven seconds apart. What had changed was their account of those things: a confused set of observations had become an ordered sequence, and an apparently urgent contradiction had become a maintenance task that could wait until morning. Niamh saved the original files beside the corrected table. Leila wrote a final note explaining what they had changed and why. Anika read it once more, caught a transposed digit in the instrument's serial number, and quietly fixed it. Before putting the notebook away, Anika asked them to follow the sequence one last time without looking at the corrected graph. She wanted to know whether the written explanation made sense on its own. Leila read the first observation. Niamh supplied the delay. Elias repeated the independent reading from the landing, including the minute at which it had been taken. At one point, the explanation referred to the second instrument without saying which instrument had been called first, so Anika replaced the phrase with eastern harbor sensor. Farther down, the word current could mean either the movement of the water or the most recent observation. She changed that sentence as well. Mara found the edits unexpectedly satisfying. Each small revision removed a place where a future reader would have needed somebody from this room to stand beside them and explain. When they reached the final line, the account could carry its own meaning. Anika placed the original notes behind it, fastened the pages together, and returned the pencil to the table. Then Elias asked whether anyone would like fresh tea.

Yes, they would.

The kettle took its time. Mara checked her recorder and found that the battery indicator had fallen to a single bar. She saved the recording, replaced the batteries, and began a new file. Before continuing, she read the time aloud: 11:38 p.m. She also named the room, the people present, and the reason for the interruption. The process felt faintly formal after so much ordinary conversation, but she wanted a future listener to understand the join. There would be a brief gap between files. There should be no gap in the account of what had happened.

Leila stood beside the sink, turning a spoon over in her hand. “People imagine this work is about exceptional moments,” she said. “Sometimes it is. Usually, though, it's about making ordinary observations carefully enough that they remain useful to someone who wasn't here.” She set the spoon down. “That someone might be a colleague tomorrow, or a student in fifty years. You don't get to choose.” Mara thought of Ruth's damaged page, its copied figures, and the four words beneath the explanation. Across the kitchen, Tomás was writing a new label for the box of cabinet handles.

Midnight arrived without ceremony. The date changed from October 15 to October 16, and Anika drew a line beneath the last entry of the day. The western windows had become mirrors. Mara could see the kitchen reflected in them, with a second table floating over the dark sea and a second Elias carrying a second tray. He placed biscuits beside the teapot. There were ginger biscuits, oat biscuits, and one exceptionally pale biscuit that he described as experimental. Tomás chose that one. A few seconds later, he moved it discreetly to the edge of his plate.

They took turns recalling the first thing they had misunderstood about the observatory. Anika had expected the telescope to be in use every night. Niamh had believed that the instrument room's three identical keys opened three different doors; in fact, all three opened the same door, and none opened the cupboard where they were stored. Tomás had spent his first afternoon looking for a missing volume that turned out to be supporting the kitchen radio. Leila's mistake had been more ambitious. She had assumed that once the paperwork was organized, it would stay organized.

Elias considered the question for a long time. When he first arrived, he said, he had believed the building was empty whenever he worked alone. Later, he began to notice how thoroughly other people remained in it. A chair had been repaired to suit a particular person's height. A pencil mark beside the pantry door recorded where somebody's child had stood. The shelving in the archive was spaced to fit a collection that had moved away years earlier. Even the path through the garden followed a route around a tree that no longer existed. “You can be the only person in a place,” he said, “and still be surrounded by decisions.”

At 12:42 a.m., Mara and Tomás returned to the archive. They were looking for a letter mentioned in Ruth's last journal, but their search led through several unrelated folders. One contained photographs of the garden in snow. Another held invoices for coal, postage, lamp oil, and a repair described only as attention to troublesome apparatus. A third folder was labeled correspondence, miscellaneous, important, perhaps. Tomás admitted that he had inherited the label. Mara suggested preserving it as a remarkably efficient summary of most correspondence. Beneath the folders they found a thin envelope addressed to R. Vale, care of the observatory.

The letter had been written by Ruth's sister. It described a missed train, a successful birthday cake, and a neighbor who had painted his front door a color everyone referred to differently. Ruth's sister called it green. Their mother called it blue. The neighbor called it duck egg and refused to discuss the matter further. Near the end, the sister asked whether the observatory was as lonely as it sounded. Ruth had drafted a reply on the back of an unused weather form. She wrote that the nights were long, the work was interesting, and the people had begun to feel familiar. She also wrote that she had learned to enjoy a room in which everyone was quietly occupied.

Mara read that sentence aloud. In the next room, Niamh was checking a cable. Upstairs, Leila was speaking softly on the telephone. Somewhere farther away, Elias opened a door and closed it again. For a moment, the sentence on the page seemed less like a description of the past than a description of the building around her. She took a photograph of the reply, including the edges of the paper and the unused columns on its reverse. Tomás asked whether she wanted the original. She shook her head. It belonged with the other pages, where the next person looking for one thing might find it by accident.

The wind eased after one o'clock. Its departure was gradual enough that nobody noticed immediately. The windows stopped trembling. The corridor seemed larger. Anika opened the inner door of the porch and listened through the closed outer door, then reported that she could hear water dripping from the gutter. Earlier, the dripping had been swallowed by everything else. Niamh checked the graph, and the latest observations supported the change. The air pressure had risen by 2.3 hectopascals over the previous ninety minutes. She wrote the figure in the log, then added a plain sentence beneath it: the worst of the local weather appears to have passed.

With the urgent work finished, fatigue became harder to ignore. Tomás read the same line three times. Leila searched for a pen she was holding. Elias poured hot water into an empty teapot and watched it emerge from the spout before remembering to close the lid. Mara's own notes grew less legible. She had written gate, blue, return beside a drawing that might have represented the coastline or the outline of a sleeping cat. The recorder, at least, continued to make its small and steady record. Its timer read 01:51:08 when she checked it, then 01:51:09, then 01:51:10.

To stay awake, Anika suggested a tour of the telescope room. They climbed a narrow staircase whose central steps had worn into shallow curves. At the top, Elias opened the heavy door and found the light switch by touch. The telescope stood beneath the dome, its brass surface dimmed by age and polished brighter where hands had rested. A wooden ladder leaned against the wall. A cabinet held lenses, notebooks, and a small sign asking visitors to avoid touching the glass. Someone had placed a knitted hat on the chair beneath the sign. The hat had a red stripe and no obvious owner.

The instrument had been built in 1912, moved to the observatory in 1936, repaired in 1961, and last used for a formal observing program in 1997. Elias could recite those dates without looking at the plaque. He was less certain about the chair, which might have arrived with the telescope or might have come from a dentist. Mara asked whether the dome still opened. “Yes,” he said. “Closing it is the part that encourages humility.” They left it closed. Through a small side window, the clouds were beginning to separate, revealing a patch of sky that looked darker than the clouds themselves.

Leila explained how a familiar constellation could disappear when she tried too hard to identify it. Looking directly at the faintest stars sometimes made them harder to see. She would shift her gaze, wait, and allow the pattern to emerge at the edge of her attention. Anika said that difficult equations occasionally behaved the same way. Niamh thought this was a generous description of the equations. Tomás said he had never encountered an equation that improved when he looked away, although he had encountered several meetings that did. Their laughter rose into the dome and returned with a small, unexpected echo.

At 3:07 a.m., they went downstairs again. Mara paused halfway along the landing to read a framed notice. It instructed observers to record unusual sounds, lights, and atmospheric effects, with sufficient detail to distinguish observation from interpretation. Below it, someone had pinned a much newer note about a suspicious glow beyond the western headland. The glow had turned out to be a fishing boat decorated for a birthday. The report remained on the wall because its author had described exactly what was visible before discovering the explanation. Leila liked it. She said it demonstrated curiosity, care, and an admirable refusal to conceal an anticlimax.

The kitchen radio could receive two stations clearly and several more as suggestions. Elias found a broadcast of quiet piano music. The announcer identified the composer, mentioned a concert in Prague, and read a message from a listener in São Paulo. Later came a piece by a musician from Reykjavík, followed by a recording made in New Orleans. Mara wrote down the place names because she liked imagining other rooms awake at the same time. A taxi office. A bakery. A hospital reception desk. A student kitchen with a single lamp. The observatory's long night belonged to a much larger collection of ordinary vigils.

At 3:54, a printer in the instrument room suddenly produced one page. Everyone looked toward the door. The machine had been silent all evening, and nobody admitted sending it anything. Niamh collected the page and discovered a test message submitted at 4:12 the previous afternoon. The delay had lasted eleven hours and forty-two minutes. At the top of the page, in large letters, was the word READY. Beneath it appeared a smaller statement confirming that the printer was connected. Niamh placed the page beside the corrected harbor readings. “Another useful lesson about time stamps,” she said.

Before dawn, Leila asked everyone to check the packing plan. Box A-17 would hold the green journals. Box A-18 would hold the blue journals and the loose sheets filed with them. The telescope accessories would travel separately, each lens in its existing case. Nothing from the wet equipment cupboard would be packed until it had dried. The kitchen clock would stay with the building, but the clock in the entrance hall belonged to Elias. This surprised Mara. He had bought it at a market for £18, repaired it with a part that cost £2.50, and spent the next nineteen years explaining that it was usually slow.

They also reviewed the signs that visitors would see during the renovation. The temporary office would open at 9:30 a.m. on weekdays. Public inquiries would go through the town library. The notice gave a contact address at observatory.example, followed by a slash and the word visits. Niamh checked the QR code with her phone. Tomás preferred the printed address because, he said, a square full of smaller squares offered him very little reassurance. Leila asked for both to remain. They corrected a missing apostrophe, expanded an ambiguous abbreviation, and changed an opening date from 11/12 to December 11.

The final page of the packing plan contained no equipment at all. It listed objects that belonged to the life of the station: Ruth's photograph, the kitchen radio, the red-striped hat, a painted wooden gull, and the small blue lantern at the northern gate. Mara had assumed the lantern was fixed permanently to the wall. Elias explained that its bracket was removable and that a new lamp would mark the temporary office. The old one would come back after the renovation. She was unexpectedly relieved. A light could move without losing the road it had helped people find.

At 5:26 a.m., the sky began to change. The first difference was almost impossible to name. The sea and the clouds, which had shared one dark surface, became separate again. A pale line appeared beyond the headland. The garden window revealed a bench, then the path, then a terracotta pot lying on its side. Mara took the recorder outside with Elias and stood beneath the covered porch. The air smelled of wet stone. Somewhere below them, a gull called once. A delivery van traveled along the distant road, its headlights moving briefly through the gap between two houses.

The blue lantern was still burning when they reached the northern gate. This time Mara approached it from the hill. Ruth had been right: from here, the center of the light looked almost white, while the blue remained at the edges. Elias opened the gate, tested the latch, and closed it again. Mara recorded twenty seconds of the morning without speaking. Then she described the light, the road, and the sound of water moving through the drain beneath the wall. She wanted the final recording to contain something a listener could recognize without having heard any of the explanations that came before it.

Back inside, breakfast was already underway. Anika had found oats. Niamh had found coffee. Tomás had found a tin of peaches whose label promised they were best enjoyed chilled, a suggestion the entire room declined. Leila read the overnight log from the beginning. She checked the sequence of calls, the corrected readings, and the note about the delayed printer message. At the bottom of the final page she wrote that the station had completed its last scheduled overnight watch before renovation. Then she signed her name and passed the book to Anika, who checked the date before signing beneath it.

Mara asked one final question. What should a person listening years from now understand about this night? Nobody answered quickly. The important events had already become difficult to separate from the smaller ones. There had been a measurement to reconcile, a letter to discover, a battery to replace, and a long stretch during which people had simply continued their work. At last, Niamh said that the record should include the uncertainty. Anika asked for the quiet parts. Tomás wanted the names spelled correctly. Elias hoped somebody would remember that he had made breakfast. Leila looked around the table and said that all four requests seemed reasonable.

At 7:02 a.m., Mara saved the final recording. She checked the first file, the middle file, and the last file, listening to a few seconds from each. The voices sounded tired in different ways, but they were all present. She packed the recorder beside her notebook and placed the photograph of Ruth in the inner pocket of her coat. Outside, the hill had become an ordinary hill again, with puddles on the path and a bus due at the shelter in twenty minutes. Elias walked her to the door. Leila waved from the kitchen. Behind them, one clock struck seven, and the other followed after a small delay.

Mara turned once at the bend in the road. The observatory stood above the garden, its windows catching the first clear light of the morning. She could no longer see the people inside, but she knew where they were. The journals were closed. The instruments were running. The kettle was on. At the northern gate, the blue lantern had finally gone out.
