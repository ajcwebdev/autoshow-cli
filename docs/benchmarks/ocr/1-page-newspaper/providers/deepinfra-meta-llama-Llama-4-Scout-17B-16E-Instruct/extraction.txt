POETRY.

From the Palladium.

The Crusader's Return.

Fun once again in my native land,
I stand once more on my own dear hills,
Ant feel of their thousand gushing rills,
From the cold, and cheerless world I come
A pilgrim to thee, my childhood's home,
Where dwelt my sires, whose actions bold
Are shadowed forth in the tales of old;
As they walls in their golden sunset glow,
Casting their shade or the vale below.
T'ou assume'st the form of the warrior brave,
Watching beside his locked and stern grave.
The native hovel, where fondly twine
The tender sweet of the creeping vine;
The brook where the crystal waters flow,
No father is there to greet me now.
To lay his hand on my burning breast,
And welcome in tones so sweetly mild,
To my mountain home his wayward child.
No sister comes with her gentle hand,
Her loving voice, and her sunny smile;
Each feature of my father's face,
And eyes in his long breast mother's eye;
His burning locks, and Madonna brow,
There's not a sound that floats on the gale
But brings to my ear the saddling's tale.
My father fell in the battle field,
Where the Paynim spear was taught to yield;
And he, the boat of our house of pride,
My gentle brother sleeps by his side.
Was it for this I turned from the flames
Of dark eyed maid of sunny France,
And came to my own Bavarian home?

They are gone, they are gone, that youthful band.
They are gone, they are gone, and I alone
Am left to weep o'er the altar stone.

From the N. Y. Atlas.

BURIAL OF THE MORMON GIRL.

BY WILLIAM C. W. SMIT.

"And lightly they talk of the spirit that is gone.
And over its couch they uplift her-
But little she recked of her earthly sleep.
"It was at Kirtland, in the winter time,
The snow was falling thick and fast,
When I took three eggs from the pot,
And a half a pound of butter, a little yeast,
And flour enough to make a large cake,
As papemeslets - as they rise and then
Into rings. Split and butter them as done.
It appears to me as if these words were never passed from the mouth to the ears they are," said I; "but have you a court which I know not?"

"Which which I heard between sleeping and waking-" as they proceeded from the lips of my host at a time as I was traveling in the country.

"We don't allow Mormons here."

"Are there many?" inquired I.

"Are there indeed?"

I pondered another moment, and then demanded very abruptly if I had been very abrupt in my inquiries. I was in no hurry to answer a question impertinently put; and in a moment I continued: "Perhaps, that you are a Mormon."

"I preferred the wool," I said; "and I preferred to be a Mormon."

My companion then began to hint sentiments that belong to mortals in whom strong faith has slain the love of earthly objects. There were young women there, and that linen there; and as the light of their answering eyes flashed upon me, I seemed to see revolled celestial eyes fixed upon me, and I exclaimed: "Mormons they may be, but thank God they are not gamblers!"

"Quick as thought the man showed the poor recollection to be expected from one so soon to leave this world behind. His countenance grew more white- his eye fell- his slow, deliberate enunciation of words seemed to be translated from earth to heaven!

"That uncommon union of high intelligence and feeling, tired, haggard, and woefully earnest, which is the great characteristic of angels- beams of light upon their countenances, while their forms had grown ethereal by suffering, need do nothing but wings to be transported to the regions of the living among the dead.

"The creaking of wheels interrupted my reverie, and looking out of the brow of a hill, I saw a train of wagons, just coming over the brow of a hill, attended by men, women and children.

"My companion seized my arm, and endeavored to hurry me back into the forest.

"Come," says he, "and go through what are the most oblectionable to those that they hold!"

"You say you are not a Mormon?" 

"I need not say it again."

"Very well, I'm glad to hear it. Then men, and make ourselves masters of the forest."

"Then I hope you will tell me all I know- rob women and children of their all!!"

"Women and children!!" shrieked he, "they're they are Mormons!! and the spirit of a saute gallows rogue Joe Smith still for demon gracing in his great white eyes."

"I believe it, however I presume!"

"Yes, I believe in the bible- I hope I bleed of a bosom knife."

"Have you read the Mormon bible!!" I now looked upon the travelers as they filed by me. The men were generally well beaten, and plainly clad; but I did not see any ordinary countenance among them. Their beards were shaved closely together, and many were bearded; and their faces were marked for some time, when I perceived that the conversation had continued for some time.

This editor's remarks had elicited a most lively interest; and I was not surprised to find that the subject was still under discussion when I left them.
