From the Palladium.
THE WANDERER.

"He had wandered through the world, and seen the most of it."

He had wandered through the world, and seen the most of it. He had been among the
people of every kind, and had known what it was to be poor and rich, and high and low, and
in a little while to be contented with either. He had seen the rise and fall of states, the
falling of kings, and the changing of dynasties; and he had lived to see that all the world is
but a stage, and that men are but actors upon it. He had also seen many strange and
marvellous things, and had heard much of the wisdom of the ancients; and yet he was not
wise enough to know that he was old.

He had a way of talking that was pleasant to hear. He could speak of the customs of
foreign countries, of the habits of men, and of the wonders of nature. He could tell of
mountains and rivers, of forests and deserts, of cities and palaces, of battles and sieges, of
storms and calms, of famine and plenty, of sorrow and joy. He had seen the lion in his
den, the elephant in his path, the tiger in his lair, the camel in his journey, the bird on his
nest, the fish in its element, and the serpent in his hole. He had heard the song of the
nightingale, the cry of the raven, the roar of the ocean, and the whisper of the wind.
And as he spoke, his eyes would brighten, and his voice would become more mellow, as
though he were again living in those scenes which he described.

He was a man of a cheerful temper, and though he had been often in danger, he had never
lost his courage. He was not one of those who are always complaining of their lot. He
had learned to bear with patience the ills of life, and to enjoy with thankfulness its blessings.
He had no home now; for the old house in which he was born had been levelled to the
ground, and the lands which his fathers tilled had passed into other hands. Yet he was not
without friends; for wherever he went, he was welcomed with kindness, and many a
hearth was gladdened by his presence.

He had a wife once, but she died young; and he had children, but they had gone before
him. He loved to speak of them, and of the happy days when they were all together; and
though tears would sometimes start into his eyes as he spoke, yet there was a sweetness in
his sorrow.

He was now returning to the place of his birth, to end his days where they began. He had
seen enough of the world to make him content to leave it. He was tired of wandering,
and desired only to lay his bones among his fathers. And as he journeyed onward, he
thought with pleasure of the quiet spot where he should sleep, and of the green turf that
would grow over him, and of the birds that would sing above his grave.
