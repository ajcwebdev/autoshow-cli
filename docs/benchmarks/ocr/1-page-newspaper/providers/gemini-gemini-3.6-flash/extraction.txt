POETRY.
From the Palladium.
The Crusader's Return.
I'm once again in my native land,
I bare my brow to its breezes bland,
I stand once more on my own dear hills,
And gaze on their thousand gushing rills.
From the cold, and cheerless world I come
A pilgrim to thee, my childhood's home,
Where dwelt my sires, whose actions bold
Are shadowed forth in the tales of old.
As thy walls in the golden sunset glow,
Casting their shade o'er the vale below,
To m'm'st the form of the warrior brave
Watching beside his loved one's grave.
The rustic bower, where fondly twine
The tendrils sweet of the creeping vine;
The brook where the chrystal waters flow,
Are here as they were ten years ago.
No father is near to greet me now,
To lay his hand on my burning brow,
And welcome in tones so sweetly mild,
To his mountain home his wayward child.
No sister comes with her gentle smile,
Her lisping voice, and her sunny smile,
Each feature of other days to trace,
And weep in her long lost mother's face.
Her auburn locks, and Madonna brow,
'Neath the churchyard mould are resting now.
There's not a sound that floats on the gale
But brings to my ear the sadd'ning tale.
My father fell in the battle field,
Where the Paynim foe was taught to yield;
And he, the boast of our house of pride,
My gentle brother sleeps by his side.
Was it for this I turned from the glance
Of the dark eyed maid of sunny France,
And came to my own Bavarian home
They are gone, they are gone, that youthful band,
The beauty and pride of my native land;
They are gone, they are gone, and I alone
Am left to weep o'er the altar stone.

To make French Muffins you must take three eggs to a pint and a half of yeast, with a small piece of butter, an egg, yeast, and flour enough to make the stiff mess as pancakes—boil it rise and then bake it like crumpets. Split and butter them while warm, — It appears to me as if these words were written by somebody who had my brain: words of small import to me, but which I heard between sleeping and waking—as they proceeded from the lips of my host's son, a day or two after my arrival in the east. It was early in the morning. I was but a board partition in the apartment in which I slept, and the one in which the family cooked their victuals. Loud talking in the adjoining apartment awakened me, and these words are remembered, together with the interesting events of the day.
Having parted with my rude, but hospitable entertainers, I took my carpet bag in my hand, and struck into a winding path, which carried me into the heart of the forest.
The birds of the wild wood carolled on every tree, and the green leaves danced cheerfully in the morning breeze. The little cataracts murmured, and the pure waters of the brook wept joyfully on in their gravelly beds. Beautiful wild flowers, which the far off citizens knew not of, sent up their perfumes. The lizard slid away among the dry leaves, and the fox drew after him his bushy tail, as I penetrated the sombre depths of the woods. Here as I was felicitating myself on the solitude that reigned around me, and admiring the harmony of Nature, I was suddenly interrupted in my contemplations by a human figure moving toward me, as if desirous of companionship.—Smoothering my resentment as well as I could, I stopped until the stranger came up. My first glance at his countenance was very unsatisfactory. Large walleye that seemed to seek for an opportunity to get some advantage over me, a long sharp chin, and thin white lips, presented a poor recommendation to my charity. That a man in the midst of so much beauty, should feel the need of a companion—that he should be willing to forego the reflections which naturally arise when green leaves and wild flowers, and whispering vines invite the fancy to try its highest flights, to enter into light conversation with me against him. The world was stamped upon his features. As he approached me he spoke.
"Well stranger, it's a fine day," It was impossible to deny the gentleman's assertion. He next stated that his way lay home, and lived but a few miles distant: that he was tired of walking in the woods, and was in a hurry to get out upon the highway.
I replied that I preferred the woods to the highway, and pointed out a most romantic sight where a number of rocks seemed to have been thrown together by some revolution of nature, and a tall pine stood upon the summit, as if to call upon the passer by to remark and ponder upon what had been done in ages long since gone by. I saw his countenance change, and I thought he lowered with his white eyebrows; I could not read the heart of a man had to force himself upon my company, and then find fault with my discourse. He was silent a moment, and then seemed ready for an effort.
"There are a great many Mormons in this neighborhood," said he.
"Are there, indeed?" returned I.
He pondered another moment. My demand seemed very abruptly if I had any dealings with that people.
I was in no hurry to answer a question so unceremoniously put, and in a moment he continued.
"Perhaps, sir, that you are a Mormon?"
"I cannot be one, until I know what a Mormon is; and it is too early for me to decide upon the creed of the sect."
"We don't allow Mormons here," remarked he, with an emphasis that double convinced me I was a Mormon or near one.
"May I ask why you do not allow people to believe their doctrine, and how you contrive to prevent it," replied I.
"We don't want them about here, and so won't have them on our lands," was his answer. "They are against everybody's religion, and Joe Smith is the greatest villain, that ever went unhung in now, stranger, you may be a Mormon for all I know: but if you are I advise you to make tracks out of this State as fast as you can go."
"What are the principal tenets of the Mormon religion?" inquired I.
"A Mormon's a Mormon," cried he in rage, and his white lip quivered; "and he ought all to be hung—the one for the sake of the other."
"I look, I merely ask for information; I have simply heard that there is such a people, and am aware that they are everywhere spoken against,—I wish to know what are the most objectionable tenets that they hold?"
"You say you are not a Mormon?"
"I need not say it again."
"Very well. I'm glad to hear it. Then you ought not to be ashamed to keep your company."
"Then I hope you will tell me all about these Mormons."
"Why, as to that," said he, "they've set a scrapion dragon's rope Joe Smith for prophet, and a golden bible, while you and now we've got one like literally, and I couldn't learn more than I could ever find time to read."
"You believe it, however, I presume?"
"Yes, I believe in the bible—I hope so."
"May I ask if you believe the whole of the book?"
"Certainly. I was always taught to believe every word of it, although I have not read it very lately, not since I was a child."
"Have you read the Mormon's bible?"
"No no stranger, I wish that their ugly beer bible were crammed into a big cannon, and I had the touching of it off."
This edifying conversation had continued for some time, when I perceived that the woods had become less dense, and the rays of the sun occasionally fell upon our path. My companion then began to hint that some exhibition or adventure was about to take place, that many of his friends would be present at it, and that I should be also welcome. As he uttered these words, there was a dark and sinister expression on his countenance—an unrelenting, hard, and worldly tone in the voice with which he regarded me—one like that which is ascribed to the eye of a hawk when he is about to pounce upon his prey. In short, he spoke mockingly, and acknowledged that he followed the profession of a gambler; and with an expression on his countenance in transferring what little loose change I had left, from my pocket to his own. All horrible creatures these Mormons must be," thought I, "when even a professed black leg, and a man who reverenced the bible, since he was a child, should feel such aversion to them."
We had now reached the landscape, and the distant hill, which opened to the view as we emerged from the forest, and presented the heavenly prospect before described. A cluster of trees grew on a gentle swell of land, which was framed by a little stream that wound around its base. It was a spot where a hermit might wish to build his hut, and pass his days in peaceful solitude. My companion here proposed that we should stop a moment and rest under the shade of a tree which stood near the water's edge. I willingly assented to the proposal, and we sat down on the grass. We had not been seated long, when we heard the sound of approaching footsteps, and looking up, I saw a train of wagons just coming over the brow of a hill, attended by men women and children. My companion seized my arm, and endeavored to hurry me back into the forest.
"Come," says he, "and go through the forest a little way, and I will show you where you will see some fun."
"I don't understand you," replied I.
"We will get together about fifty stout men, and make ourselves masters of the emigrants' wagons," said he.
"What!" cried I, "would you rob women and children?"
"Women and children!" shrieked he, with rage, and looking up, I saw a train of wagons just coming over the brow of a hill, attended by men, women, and children. My companion seized my arm, and endeavored to hurry me back into the forest.
"Come," says he, "and go through the forest a little way, and I will show you where you will see some fun."
Quick as thought the man showed the blade of a bowie knife; and then I drew my revolver, and looking up, I saw a train of wagons just coming over the brow of a hill. His countenance grew more white—his eye fell—he slowly backed off from me, until he was hidden from my sight among the bushes. I remembered the words, "Resist the devil and he will flee from thee."
I now looked out upon the travellers as their wagons filed past me. The men were generally well-proportioned, and plainly clad; but I could not see an ordinary countenance among them. Their bearing was dignified and manly. They talked together with an air of high intelligence, and radiated with all those benign and lofty sentiments that belong to mortals in whom strong faith has slain the love of earthly objects. There were young women there; and as the light of their angelic eyes flashed upon me, I seemed to be translated from earth to heaven! That uncommon union of high intelligence with perfect innocence which is the great characteristic of angels—beamed upon their countenances, while their forms which had grown ethereal by suffering, needed nothing but wings to be true representatives, of the celestial and radiant hosts who watch over the peace of the good on earth! My heart bled for the hunted and the outraged martyrs of the present age. Those are the pilgrims upon the earth who seek a city not built with hands; and why—why then should it not hate those who have come out from it? Why should not such worthies as the blackleg—the worldly profit seeking—howling wolves in pursuing a people who have risen and shaken themselves from the dust of the earth?
These thoughts passed through my mind as the train moved slowly on, until I arrived at the spot where they had formed a circle and were gathered around some object in their midst. Curiosity prompted me to press forward, and I saw a plain pine box lying upon the green sward. The cover was off and the box lay that most delicately beautiful form that I ever beheld.
