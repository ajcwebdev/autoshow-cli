## Title

Inside React Miami Month: How a Game Show Format Is Reinventing Tech Conference Content

## Outline

- Introduction: Setting the scene - a Twitter Spaces hiccup and the kickoff of a special collaboration
- Background: JavaScript Jam Live as an open mic for the web development community
- The React Miami partnership: Who's involved and why it matters
- Miami Jam: Reimagining speaker interviews as an interactive game show
- Why engagement and community participation drive better conversations
- Implications for the future of developer events and content
- Conclusion and call to action for community participation

## Draft

Every online event organizer has a story about technology betraying them at the worst possible moment, and the recent JavaScript Jam Live session kicking off React Miami Month was no exception. The Space stubbornly refused to go live for everyone else even though it appeared to be running fine on the host's phone. But as host Anthony Campolo noted, sometimes patience really is a virtue: after a minute or two of waiting, the platform sorted itself out, and the show went on. That small kerfuffle turned into an impromptu lesson for Twitter Spaces hosts everywhere, and it set an appropriately human, unscripted tone for what turned out to be an exciting announcement-packed session. For the uninitiated, JavaScript Jam Live describes itself as an open mic for anything and everything related to JavaScript and web development. The hosts, including VP of Product at Edgio Ishan Anand, developer advocate Anthony Campolo, and technical community manager Scott Steinlage, have built the show around audience participation. Whether you have been writing code for two decades or two weeks, you are welcome to raise a hand, come up to the mic, and share questions, opinions, or hot takes. That ethos matters because some of the best conversations happen when community members get involved, and the crew makes it a point to say so explicitly. This particular episode carried extra weight because it marked the beginning of what Scott Steinlage dubbed React Miami Month, a multi-week collaboration between the JavaScript Jam crew and the organizers of React Miami, a growing conference in Miami focused on the React ecosystem. Michelle, one of the conference organizers, joined the Space to express her excitement and to reveal that the conference had essentially handed the media partners a green light to create whatever content they wanted around the event. Alongside Anthony and Scott, media partner Dev Agrawal joined from his own space in the ecosystem, and the group hinted that more React Miami speakers would appear on future shows. So what does a green light from a major conference look like in the hands of developer advocates? The centerpiece is a show called Miami Jam, and its premise is deceptively simple: instead of running yet another standard interview or panel where speakers recite their talk abstracts, Miami Jam turns the conversation into a trivia-style game show. The questions span everything from React fundamentals to weird quirks in JavaScript and TypeScript, adjacent libraries, and even questions about what other speakers plan to discuss. Dev Agrawal, who conceived the format, explained that each trivia question deliberately segues into deeper discussion. If a question touches on a speaker's conference topic, it becomes a launchpad for a real conversation about their motivations, their industry experience, and the projects that brought them to Miami in the first place. The guests still walk away having shared genuine insight; the audience just has a lot more fun along the way. The game show mechanics do not stop at the speakers. Audience members are woven directly into the action. Beyond the usual opportunity to ask questions, listeners can be assigned power over the game itself, for instance deciding how many points a speaker earns for a given answer. That may sound like a small gimmick, but it reflects a deliberate philosophy about how developer communities function best. When audiences are passive, events feel like lectures. When audiences have agency, even in something as lighthearted as awarding points, the entire dynamic shifts toward collaboration. The hosts repeatedly emphasized that value compounds when more people come up to the mic, and the Miami Jam format is designed to lower the barrier to doing exactly that. The unpolished charm of the session was part of its appeal. Michelle fought a flaky connection and a microphone button that refused to cooperate before finally getting her introduction out. Scott joked about how no amount of technical knowledge immunizes anyone from occasionally looking like a fool in front of an audience. One participant, Dan, confessed he would unfortunately miss the conference entirely due to a company offsite scheduled for the same week. These moments read like bloopers, but they are actually the substance of community-driven media: real people solving real problems in real time, with enough self-awareness to laugh about it. There is a Demotivators-style joke hidden in the opening kerfuffle too, with one host referencing the famous poster about the Titanic and the line that maybe your role is to be a lesson for others. That kind of humor is the connective tissue that makes technical communities feel less like anonymous forums and more like a group of friends. The implications of this collaboration extend beyond one trivia show. Conferences have historically struggled to produce engaging pre-event and post-event content that reaches people who cannot attend in person. By empowering media partners to experiment with formats like game shows, conferences like React Miami can extend their reach into the audio-first Twitter Spaces audience while giving community builders room to innovate. The model is worth watching for anyone who organizes developer events: give community-savvy people creative freedom, and they will produce content that feels like entertainment while still teaching substantive material. For the JavaScript Jam crew, the weeks ahead promise more speakers, more audience participation, and more of the same energy that defines their open mic format. For listeners, invitations or not, the call to action is simple: whether you are a beginner or an advanced user, come up, ask questions, and shape the conversation. The hosting team leaves the door open to everyone, and Miami Jam is a direct result of what happens when a community takes that invitation seriously. React Miami Month is officially underway, and if the opening session is any indication, it will be equal parts educational chaos and technical insight, with points awarded at the audience's discretion. If you care about React, about JavaScript, or about the future of genuinely participatory tech media, this is one Spaces series to keep on your radar.
