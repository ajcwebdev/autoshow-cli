## Draft

## When the Space Doesn't Start

There's a particular flavor of dread that only live audio hosts know. You tap the button. Your phone says you're live. You start talking. And absolutely nobody can hear you.

That's exactly how a recent episode of JavaScript Jam Live began. The Twitter Space showed as running on the host's device while remaining invisible to everyone else — no listeners, no joiners, just a broadcaster talking into a void. The fix, as co-host Anthony Campolo explained once things finally stabilized, was almost comically simple: wait. "Sometimes when you start a Space, it will show as starting on your phone while having not actually been started where other people can see it and join it. But if you wait like one to two minutes, somehow it just works it out and then all of a sudden it goes live."

Patience, in other words, was the virtue of the hour. Co-host Ishan Anand summed up the mood with a nod to the old Demotivators poster featuring the Titanic: *maybe your role is to be a lesson for others.*

It's a small moment, but it sets the tone for everything that followed — because the episode turned out to be about exactly this: building live, messy, human-scale developer events where the imperfection is part of the appeal.

## Who's In the Room

JavaScript Jam Live describes itself as an open mic for anything JavaScript and web development related. The hosting trio comes out of Edgio: Ishan Anand (VP of Product), Anthony Campolo (developer advocate), and Scott Steinlage (technical community manager). Joining them were Dev Agrawal — who answers to Dev or Dave, both fine — and Michelle, one of the organizers behind React Miami, who dropped in mid-stream after her own microphone gremlins resolved themselves.

The news of the episode: Anthony, Scott, and Dev are official media partners for React Miami. And rather than treating that as a logo-on-a-slide arrangement, they're turning it into a full month of programming.

Scott caught himself announcing "React Miami Week" before correcting course live on air: "Really it's React Miami *Month*, which I'm renaming right now." Upcoming episodes will feature React Miami speakers talking about the topics they're bringing to the conference — all while keeping the show's existing rhythm intact. As Scott put it, nothing's going to change about what listeners are used to hearing. The partnership is additive: more speakers, more access, more value, plus "some extra goodies" the team teased for later.

## The Open Mic Is the Point

If there's a thesis running through JavaScript Jam, it's that the audience isn't an audience. Scott made the pitch explicitly and more than once: whether you're a beginner or you've been building for the web for decades, come up. Ask a question. Offer an opinion. Drop a fact. Disagree.

And the reasoning isn't purely altruistic — it's a practical observation about what makes live shows good. When listeners step up to the mic, the conversation gets better for everyone. Prepared remarks are fine. Unprepared, genuine curiosity is often better. A show that only ever hears from its hosts starts to sound like a podcast with extra steps.

This is a lesson a lot of developer-facing content misses. The default instinct is to polish, script, and control. But the format that keeps people returning week after week is the one where something unscripted might happen — including, apparently, the show failing to start.

## Enter Miami Jam

The centerpiece announcement was Dev's brainchild: **Miami Jam**, a game show he's roped Anthony and Scott into co-hosting at React Miami.

The motivation is a critique most of us have felt but rarely articulate. Conference interview panels tend to follow a script: bring up three speakers, ask what their talk is about, ask what inspired it, thank them, repeat. It's informative. It's also forgettable.

Miami Jam swaps the interview for trivia. Instead of asking speakers to summarize their own talks, the hosts will fire off trivia-style questions — React fundamentals, weird quirks of JavaScript and TypeScript, anything in the React-adjacent orbit. Some questions might be "guess which library this is." Others might be about *another* speaker's topic, directed at someone who has to take a swing at it.

The genius is in what happens next. Each question becomes a segue. A trivia answer about a TypeScript edge case opens the door to a real conversation about why that speaker cares, what they've built, and what brought them to Miami. As Dev framed it, the hosts still get to pull out plenty of insight — the speaker's motivation, their experience in the industry, the work behind the talk — but the path there is more fun to walk.

There's a secondary benefit too: speakers end up sharing experiences with *each other*, not just performing for the room. A panel puts speakers in parallel. A game show puts them in conversation.

## Giving the Audience a Lever

The most interesting design choice is what Miami Jam gives the live audience. Beyond the standard ability to ask questions, attendees will get to influence the game itself. Dev's example: when a question is posed, the audience can decide how many points answering it is worth.

That's a small mechanic with outsized effects. It transfers real stakes to the people in the seats. It creates a feedback loop where the crowd's interest directly shapes the show's momentum. And it means attending isn't a passive activity — your vote changes the outcome.

This is the same philosophy that powers the open mic on the podcast, just translated into a room with a stage.

## Trust as an Organizing Principle

One detail from Michelle deserves attention. Asked what React Miami expects from its media partners, she said the organizers essentially "gave them the green light to do whatever they like" — and that she was tuning in partly out of genuine curiosity about what the team would come up with. Anthony's deadpan response: "We'll see whether you end up regretting it or not."

That exchange is a quiet lesson for event organizers. The temptation with partner content is to approve, template, and brand-guideline it into safety. React Miami took the opposite bet: find people who already understand the community, then get out of their way. The result is a game show that nobody would have arrived at through a committee process.

## What to Take Away

A few things worth carrying into your own work:

**Build for recovery, not perfection.** The Space failed. The hosts narrated the failure, thanked the audience for rejoining, made a joke about the Titanic, and kept going. Nobody left. Transparency about breakage builds more trust than pretending it didn't happen.

**Lower the bar to participate.** Saying "we want to hear from everybody" once is a nicety. Saying it three times in two minutes, and explaining *why* it makes the show better, is an invitation people act on.

**Question your format defaults.** The interview panel exists because it's easy to organize, not because it's the best way to learn about someone's work. Trivia, games, live voting, and collaborative formats cost more effort upfront and pay back in engagement.

**Hand over the keys.** The most memorable partner content comes from organizers who trust their collaborators with creative latitude rather than a content calendar.

## What's Next

React Miami Month is underway, with more speakers set to appear on JavaScript Jam Live in the weeks ahead and Miami Jam landing at the conference itself. If the first episode is any indication, the programming will be a mix of genuine technical depth, improvised comedy, and at least one piece of software not working as advertised.

That last part isn't a bug. In live developer community work, it might be the most relatable feature there is.
