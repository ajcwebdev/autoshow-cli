# Miami Jam: Bringing Game-Show Energy to React Developer Conversations

Developer events often follow a familiar pattern: introduce a speaker, ask about their work, discuss their conference talk, and leave a few minutes for audience questions. That format can be useful, but it is not the only way to share technical knowledge.

In an April 2023 JavaScript Jam Live conversation about React Miami, the hosts and their guest, Dev Agrawal, introduced an alternative: Miami Jam, a planned game-show-style event built around technical trivia, speaker conversations, and audience involvement.

The idea was not to replace substance with entertainment. It was to use a more playful structure to help people connect with the substance—and with one another.

## A Live Event Begins With a Very Familiar Problem

Before the discussion reached React or JavaScript, the hosts had to work through trouble with Twitter Spaces. The session appeared to be starting on a host’s phone without becoming available for other people to join.

Anthony Campolo explained that he had encountered similar behavior before: sometimes waiting a minute or two allowed the Space to become available. Scott Steinlage, who had worked to get the session running, laughed about the experience. Technical knowledge, he observed, does not prevent technology from making you look foolish occasionally.

The exchange set an appropriately informal tone. This was a developer gathering where imperfect tools and awkward moments were part of the experience, not something to hide.

That matters for community events. When hosts acknowledge problems without becoming defensive, they can make the room feel more approachable. Beginners are not the only people who encounter confusing software behavior; experienced developers and community organizers do too.

## Keeping the JavaScript Jam Open-Mic Spirit

Ishan Anand, introduced as Edgio’s VP of Product, described JavaScript Jam Live as an open mic for JavaScript and web development. Anthony, a developer advocate at Edgio, and Scott, Edgio’s technical community manager, joined him in hosting the conversation.

Audience participation was central to their description of the show. Scott explicitly welcomed people across experience levels, inviting questions, comments, facts, and opinions from both beginners and advanced developers.

His point was not simply that everyone was allowed to speak. It was that participation made the discussion more valuable for everyone listening. A question from the audience can reveal a confusing assumption, introduce a practical concern, or take a conversation somewhere the hosts had not anticipated.

The planned React Miami collaboration was intended to preserve that atmosphere. Scott explained that upcoming sessions would feature more React Miami speakers discussing relevant topics while maintaining the familiar JavaScript Jam style.

He even jokingly expanded the framing from React Miami “Week” to React Miami “Month,” reflecting the broader run of conversations the hosts were anticipating.

That distinction is important. The excerpt captures a concept being introduced, not evidence of how the event ultimately performed. What it does provide is a clear explanation of the format and the goals behind it.

## Trivia as an Entry Point, Not the Destination

Dev’s central proposal was to move away from asking speakers only direct questions about their topics and motivations. Instead, Miami Jam would begin with trivia-style prompts.

Possible subjects included React, unusual JavaScript or TypeScript behavior, and other topics adjacent to the React ecosystem. Participants might be asked to identify a library or answer a question connected to another speaker’s presentation.

The key was what would happen after the answer.

A question would create an opening for a more detailed discussion about a speaker’s subject, their reasons for giving a particular talk, or the industry experience that informed their work. In that structure, trivia serves as a conversational starting point rather than a final test of expertise.

For example, a question about a surprising language behavior could lead naturally into an explanation of why developers encounter it and how it affects their work. That is an illustration of the format’s potential, not a specific exchange documented in the excerpt.

The broader principle is straightforward: a short, engaging challenge can create curiosity that a longer explanation then satisfies.

## Giving the Audience More Than a Q&A Slot

Miami Jam was also intended to encourage speakers to exchange experiences with one another. Rather than having every answer flow back to a moderator, the format aimed to make the conversation more collaborative.

The audience would have a role beyond listening. Dev said attendees would be able to ask questions and might participate in other ways, including deciding how many points a speaker received for an answer.

The supplied excerpt ends while he is still explaining audience involvement, so it does not establish a complete scoring system or final set of rules. Still, the direction is clear: audience members were being considered as contributors to the event itself.

That represents a useful shift in event design. Participation need not be confined to a few minutes at the end. It can be built into the structure of the session.

## Making Playfulness Work for Learning

A game-show format also presents a design challenge. If a community welcomes beginners, competition should not make people feel embarrassed about what they do not know.

The hosts did not lay out detailed safeguards in this excerpt, but their emphasis on inclusion suggests a useful standard for anyone adapting the idea: reward explanations and curiosity, not just fast answers.

Organizers could vary question difficulty, invite speakers to reason through answers together, and treat mistakes as openings for clarification. These are recommendations inspired by the proposed format, rather than confirmed Miami Jam rules.

The same applies to pacing. Too much trivia could leave little room for insight; too much uninterrupted explanation could erase the energy that makes the format distinctive. The strongest version would give each prompt enough discussion time to become useful.

## A Different Way to Connect

The Miami Jam proposal illustrates how developer communities can experiment without abandoning their educational purpose. Technical questions, speaker experiences, and audience contributions can coexist within a lighter, more interactive structure.

The opening technical hiccups reinforced the same human quality the planned event was aiming for: knowledgeable people sharing a space, figuring things out, and inviting others into the conversation.

The takeaway is not that every developer panel needs points or trivia. It is that format should serve participation. When an event helps people ask questions, exchange experiences, and explore the reasoning behind an answer, it can offer more than a sequence of presentations. It can become a shared learning experience.
