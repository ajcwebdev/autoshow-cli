CHAPTER II

The Astrolabe and the Open Sea

By the early fifteenth century the mariners of Portugal had begun to
ven-
ture beyond the sight of land, trusting instruments their grandfathers
would have called witchcraft.1 The astrolabe, in particular, trans-
formed the practice of celestial navigation, allowing a pilot to fix his
latitude from the height of the sun at noon.

Prince Henry, whom later writers would call the Navigator, gathered
at Sagres a company of cartographers and ship wrights whose work
would outlast their names.2

1 The word derives from the Greek astrolabos, "star-taker." See Taylor,
The Haven-Finding Art (1956), pp. 127-131.
2 Sagres was never the formal "school" of romantic legend; the term is a
later embellishment.
